    Guide · LVP in a basement

    Quick answer

    Can you put LVP in a basement?

    Usually, yes. Floating rigid-core vinyl plank is a common basement pick because the plank doesn't swell when it gets wet. But the plank is only half the floor. The concrete under it has to pass a moisture test, be flat enough, and get the vapor barrier your brand calls for.

    Why basements are different

    • The slab breathes moisture. Concrete below grade can pass water vapor up from the ground, even when it looks dry.
    • Waterproof planks still trap water. Vinyl doesn't stop moisture coming up from the slab. Water that gets under the floor stays there, and that is where mold grows.
    • Warranties have conditions. Many install sheets say the warranty does not cover flooding, slab moisture, or hydrostatic pressure (water pushed up from below).

    Step 1: test the slab for moisture

    1. Start with the plastic sheet test — tape an 18 × 18 in sheet of clear plastic tightly to the bare slab, all four edges sealed, out of direct sun. Leave it at least 16 hours. Droplets under the plastic, or darker concrete under it, mean stop and fix the moisture first. This is ASTM D4263.
    2. Know its limit — a pass on the plastic test only means no surface moisture that day. It is a yes/no check, not a measurement.
    3. Do the test your brand names — many install sheets ask for in-slab relative humidity probes (ASTM F2170) or a calcium chloride test (ASTM F1869). A common limit is under 90% RH or 8 lbs per 1,000 sq ft per 24 hours, but some products want 80–85% RH or 5–6 lbs. Your sheet wins.
    4. Test more than one spot — install sheets often ask for about 3 tests for the first 1,000 sq ft, including near outside walls. Write the results down. Some warranties ask for them.
    5. New concrete waits — several makers want a new slab to cure at least 90 days before flooring goes down.
    Stop if: water comes in when it rains, the slab has standing water or white crusty deposits that keep coming back, or the floor slopes to a drain. Fix the grading, gutters, or drainage first, or call a basement waterproofing pro. A new floor hides the problem. It doesn't fix it.

    Step 2: control the room's humidity

    The EPA suggests keeping indoor humidity below 60%, ideally 30–50%. A cheap hygrometer (the EPA puts them at about $10–$50) tells you where your basement sits. If it stays damp all summer, run a dehumidifier before and after the floor goes in.

    Step 3: vapor barrier and underlayment

    6 mil poly vapor barrier

    Many brands require a 6 mil polyethylene sheet over concrete. Overlap seams and tape them, and run it a couple of inches up the wall, then trim after the floor is in.

    Attached pad

    Not a fix for a wet slab

    A poly sheet slows vapor. It doesn't make a slab that fails the moisture test okay.

    High readings need drying time or a mitigation system rated for flooring.

    Flatness matters on concrete too. Many brands want about 3/16 in over 10 ft. Grind high spots and fill low spots with a cement-based patch or leveler. See Prep for the full checklist.

    Which LVP works best below grade

    • Rigid-core click-lock (SPC or WPC): the usual pick. It floats, bridges small slab flaws, and can be lifted after a leak.
    • Glue-down: works on a good slab, but the glue often has its own moisture limit, and planks rarely come up whole after a flood. Treat it as advanced. See Glue-down vs click-lock.
    • Thin flexible or peel-and-stick: shows every bump in the concrete and depends on the slab staying dry.
    • Laminate, carpet, or solid wood: these swell or soak up water. LVP is the usual pick over a slab that may get damp. See LVP vs laminate.

    Pick the wear layer the same way as upstairs: about 12 mil for a normal family room, and 20 mil or more for dogs, a home gym, or a rental. See Understand LVP.

    If the basement floods

    1. Stop the water and stay safe — shut off power to wet areas if you can do it safely. If the water was sewage or outside floodwater, call a pro. Those planks and pads are usually thrown out, not cleaned.
    2. Lift the floor fast — a floating floor comes up in reverse order, starting from the last row. Number the backs of the planks so they go back in the same spot.
    3. Dry everything within 24–48 hours — that is the EPA's window to prevent mold. Use fans and a dehumidifier. Throw out wet foam underlayment.
    4. Re-test before relaying — the slab has to pass the same moisture limit as a new install. Clean, undamaged planks can often go back down. Chipped locks won't hold.

    A floating floor's biggest basement advantage is that you can take it up. Keep a box of spare planks for repairs, and check your homeowners insurance for what it covers after water damage.

    What a basement floor costs

    Basement LVP costs roughly the same per square foot as upstairs, plus moisture work. Cost guides put installed basement LVP at roughly $4–$10 per sq ft, and Angi's 2026 basement guide puts most basement flooring at $3–$10. Budget extra for:

    • Vapor barrier or barrier underlayment: about $0.50–$1.50 per sq ft.
    • Slab leveling or patching: about $1–$3 per sq ft where needed.
    • A dehumidifier if the room runs damp, and any drainage or waterproofing fixes first. Those are separate jobs with their own quotes.

    LVP in a basement FAQ

    Can you put vinyl plank flooring in a basement?

    Usually, yes. Floating rigid-core LVP is a common basement pick because the plank does not swell when it gets wet. The concrete slab still has to pass the moisture test your plank maker names, be flat enough, and get the vapor barrier the install sheet calls for.

    How do I test a basement slab for moisture before LVP?

    A quick check is to tape an 18 by 18 inch sheet of clear plastic tightly to the slab for at least 16 hours. Damp concrete or droplets under it means stop. That only shows surface moisture, so many plank makers ask for a measured test instead: in-slab relative humidity probes (ASTM F2170) or a calcium chloride test (ASTM F1869). Compare the result with the limit on your install sheet.

    Do I need a vapor barrier under LVP in a basement?

    Often, yes. Many brands require a 6 mil polyethylene vapor barrier over concrete, with seams overlapped and taped. Some planks or underlayments include a barrier already. Follow the install sheet for your exact product, and don't stack a second pad under an attached one unless the brand allows it.

    Is waterproof LVP okay if my basement floods?

    The planks survive water, but the floor as a whole does not stop it. Water gets under through seams and edges and gets trapped. A floating floor can be lifted so the slab can dry. The EPA says to dry wet areas within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold. Most warranties do not cover flood damage.

    How much does basement LVP cost?

    Roughly $4 to $10 per square foot installed in many basements, plus about $0.50 to $1.50 per square foot if you need a vapor barrier or barrier underlayment, and about $1 to $3 per square foot where the slab needs leveling. These are approximate 2026 ranges.
