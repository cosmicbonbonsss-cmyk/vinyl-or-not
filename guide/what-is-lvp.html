    Chapter 2

    What is LVP flooring?

    Luxury vinyl plank (LVP) is a multi-layer floor that looks like wood (or stone) but is mostly vinyl and a rigid or flexible core. Knowing the layers — and where thin products fail — keeps you from buying the wrong box for the room.

    What LVP actually is

    From the top down you usually have: a clear wear layer, a printed design film, a core (the stiff “body”), and often an attached foam pad on the bottom. Two numbers on the box measure different things. Total thickness is the whole plank, listed in millimeters (most home click-lock LVP is about 4 to 8 mm). The wear layer is only the clear top coat, listed in mils (1 mil is a thousandth of an inch). A 20 mil wear layer is about half a millimeter, so “12” or “20” on a box almost always means mils, not millimeters.

    Rigid core vs flexible

    Rigid core (SPC / WPC)

    SPC (stone plastic / rigid polymer) and WPC (wood plastic) cores stay flatter, dent less under chairs, and are the usual DIY click-lock choice today. SPC is typically denser and more stable; WPC can feel a bit warmer underfoot.

    Flexible vinyl

    Older or budget glue-down / loose-lay sheets and planks bend easily. They can look great when installed well, but they need a flatter, cleaner substrate and are less forgiving for first-time DIY than rigid click-lock.

    Wear layers (mils) — use cases

    • ~6 mil — light residential, guest rooms, low traffic. Scratches show sooner with pets or grit.
    • ~12 mil — normal home use and kids. A solid DIY sweet spot for living areas.
    • ~20 mil+ — dogs, high traffic, rental turnovers, light commercial-adjacent. Premium durability.

    Wear layer protects the print; it does not make a thin core behave like a thick one under heavy loads.

    Total plank thickness (mm) — and where each fails

    Box labels advertise total thickness in millimeters. Thicker rigid cores usually feel better and hide minor subfloor waves better — but no residential LVP is magic. Pick the wear layer (in mils, above) separately. Here’s the honest take:

    ~4 mm budget

    Entry-level rigid or older flexible builds. Fine for low-traffic bedrooms if the subfloor is excellent.

    Fails in: garages (temperature swings + vehicle loads); also weak long-term in busy family homes — dents, broken locks, telegraphing.

    ~5–6 mm midrange

    Common midrange DIY. Comfortable for most homes when wear layer and prep are solid.

    Can struggle under heavy furniture, rolling loads (office chairs, carts), or very high-traffic spaces.

    ~6–8 mm premium rigid core

    Premium SPC/WPC rigid core. Safer match for active family homes, larger rooms, and slightly imperfect (but still in-spec) subfloors.

    Still follow flatness and moisture rules — thicker does not forgive a wet slab.

    ~8–9 mm with attached pad

    The top of the home range. The attached foam pad makes up part of that number, so it feels softer and quieter underfoot.

    Choose when you want the most comfort and margin — not a substitute for garage-rated products.
    What buying too thin looks like: you see and feel ridges from the subfloor (telegraphing), click joints that crack or won’t stay locked, chair dents, edges that peak or cup, and the printed design wearing through early in traffic lanes.
    Garages: standard residential LVP is usually the wrong category. Cold/hot swings, tire loads, and oil call for products made for garages (or epoxy / specialty systems) — not a 6 mm living-room plank. When in doubt, ask the manufacturer if the SKU is garage-rated before you buy.

    Waterproof vs water-resistant

    “Waterproof” usually means the plank won’t swell like laminate or hardwood when wet. It does not mean floods are fine, or that water trapped under a floating floor is harmless. Edges, transitions, and the subfloor still matter. Standing water can ruin trim, walls, and warranties.

    Click-lock vs glue-down

    • Click-lock (floating) — planks lock together and “float” over the subfloor with an expansion gap at walls. Best DIY path; covered in the Install chapter.
    • Glue-down — adhesive bonds the vinyl to the substrate. More unforgiving (open time, trowel size, flatness). Treat it as advanced / pro territory unless the brand’s DIY guide is crystal clear and you’ve done it before.

    Underlayment: attached vs separate

    Many rigid planks ship with foam already glued on. Don’t add a second thick pad unless the manufacturer allows it — double padding can void warranties and make locks fail. Separate underlayment or a vapor barrier is used when the brand requires it (especially over concrete). Always match the written install instructions for that SKU.

    LVP basics FAQ

    What do SPC and WPC mean on vinyl plank?

    They describe the core. SPC is a stone plastic composite, a dense rigid core. WPC is a wood plastic composite, also rigid, and it can feel a bit softer and warmer underfoot. Both are common for DIY click-lock floors.

    Is LVP waterproof?

    The plank itself does not swell when wet, which is why boxes say waterproof. That does not make the room waterproof. Water can get through seams and edges to the subfloor, so wipe up spills and fix leaks.

    What wear layer should I get for LVP?

    About 6 mil is for light use, about 12 mil is the usual home floor, and 20 mil and up is the pick for dogs and heavy traffic. The wear layer is measured in mils, which is not the same as the total plank thickness in millimeters.

    Is LVP the same as laminate?

    No. LVP is made of vinyl layers over a vinyl-based core. Laminate has a wood-fiber core under a printed image and a hard clear top. See LVP vs laminate for how they compare.
