    Quick answer

    Glue-down vs click-lock LVP: which should you pick?

    For most homes and most DIYers, click-lock. It floats, goes in faster, needs no adhesive, and comes back up after a leak. Glue-down makes sense for big open areas, heavy rolling loads, and very stable commercial-style floors, but it needs a smoother subfloor and more skill.

    How each one works

    Click-lock (floating)

    Planks have a tongue-and-groove locking edge. You angle or tap each one into the last, and the whole floor floats over the subfloor with a small gap at the walls. Nothing is glued or nailed. Most rigid-core (SPC and WPC) planks work this way, often with a pad attached.

    Glue-down (dry-back)

    Thinner, flexible planks with flat edges. You spread a pressure-sensitive adhesive with a notched trowel, wait for it to get tacky, lay the planks into it, then roll the whole floor with a heavy roller so every plank bonds. The floor can't move or be lifted cleanly.

    Cost

    • Planks: glue-down planks are often a bit cheaper per square foot, because they are thinner and have no locking edge.
    • Labor: glue-down usually costs more. One installer's cost guide puts pro labor at about $2–$4 per sq ft for glue-down and about $1.50–$3 for click-lock. Local quotes are often higher. See LVP cost per square foot for wider ranges.
    • Adhesive and tools: about $0.30–$0.75 per sq ft for adhesive, plus the right trowel and a 100 lb roller, which you would usually rent.
    • Prep: glue-down needs a smoother floor, so patching or leveling costs often go up.
    • DIY savings: realistic with click-lock. With glue-down, mistakes are glued in place.

    Where each fits

    Pick click-lock for

    Bedrooms, living rooms, kitchens, and most basements. First-time DIY jobs. Going over existing flat tile. Anywhere you may need to lift the floor after a leak.

    The default for most homes.

    Pick glue-down for

    Large open areas with no room for transitions. Heavy rolling loads like office chairs or carts. Commercial-style spaces. Any spot where your plank's install sheet calls for full-spread glue.

    Usually a pro install.

    Basements

    Click-lock is usually easier. It can come up after a flood, and the slab can dry. Glue-down adhesive has its own moisture limit to check.

    Over old floors

    Click-lock can often float over flat, solid tile. Glue-down needs a surface the adhesive can bond to, which usually means more prep.

    DIY difficulty

    Click-lock needs a tapping block, pull bar, spacers, and a cutter or utility knife. The skills are layout, staggering seams, and the expansion gap. See the install chapter.

    Glue-down adds steps that are easy to get wrong:

    1. Prep the floor — smooth, clean, and dry. Many brands want about 1/8 in over 6 ft. Dust, old glue, or bumps can show through thin planks.
    2. Spread the adhesive — with the exact trowel notch on the adhesive label. Too much glue can ooze up between planks.
    3. Wait for tack — many pressure-sensitive adhesives must dry to tack before the planks go down. Spread only what you can cover within the working time.
    4. Lay and roll — roll the whole floor with a 75–100 lb roller, often in both directions. Keep traffic light for about 24 hours.

    Subfloor needs

    • Both: sound, clean, dry, and tested for moisture over concrete. See Prep.
    • Click-lock: flat within the brand's spec. Small texture is hidden by the pad. A vapor barrier is often required over concrete.
    • Glue-down: flat and smooth. Fill cracks and holes with a cement-based patch. Some adhesives want porous floors primed first.

    Removal and repairs later

    A floating click-lock floor comes up plank by plank, and planks can often be reused. A damaged plank near a wall is a quick swap. Glue-down planks usually break coming up, and the adhesive has to be scraped off. One flooring contractor's removal guide prices glued-down vinyl at roughly $2–$3 per sq ft, compared with about $1–$2 for floating vinyl. In older homes, old vinyl and its glue can contain asbestos, so test before you scrape.

    Glue-down vs click-lock FAQ

    Is glue-down or click-lock LVP better?

    For most homes and most DIYers, click-lock. It floats, goes in faster, needs no adhesive, and can be lifted after a leak. Glue-down is better for large open areas, heavy rolling loads, and commercial-style spaces where a fully bonded, very stable floor matters, as long as the subfloor is smooth and flat.

    Is glue-down LVP cheaper than click-lock?

    The planks often are, because glue-down planks are thinner and have no locking edge. The install usually is not. One installer's 2026 cost guide puts pro labor at about $2 to $4 per square foot for glue-down versus about $1.50 to $3 for click-lock, and adhesive adds about $0.30 to $0.75 per square foot. Local quotes vary, so treat these as approximate.

    Can a beginner install glue-down LVP?

    It is possible but harder. You have to spread adhesive with the right trowel, wait the right time before laying planks, work within the adhesive's working time, and roll the floor with a heavy roller. The subfloor also has to be smoother and flatter. Most DIYers start with click-lock.

    Which is easier to remove or repair?

    Click-lock. A floating floor comes apart plank by plank and can often be relaid. Glue-down planks usually break coming up, and the adhesive has to be scraped off the subfloor, so removal costs more and single-plank repairs take more work.

    Does glue-down LVP need underlayment?

    Usually no. Glue-down planks bond straight to a smooth, clean subfloor or approved underlayment panel. Click-lock planks often have an attached pad or go over a thin underlayment, and many brands want a vapor barrier over concrete. Follow the install sheet for your product.
