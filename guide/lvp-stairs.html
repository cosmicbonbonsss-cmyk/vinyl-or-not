    Quick answer

    How to put vinyl plank (LVP) on stairs

    Yes, you can put vinyl plank on stairs, but it doesn't float there. Every tread piece, riser, and stair nose gets glued down, and the stair nose you pick sets the look and the difficulty. Expect it to take longer and cost more per square foot than a flat floor.

    How stairs are different from floors

    • Glued, not floating. Stair install sheets call for gluing the planks and the nosing, usually with a polyurethane construction adhesive.
    • Pad comes off. Many brands say to remove the attached pad from planks used on stairs.
    • Every step is custom. Measure and cut each tread and riser on its own. Stairs are rarely all the same size.
    • The nose is a separate piece. Use the stair nose made to match your plank, in the profile that fits your stairs.

    Stair nosing options

    Matching nosing from the flooring maker gives the best color match. Many vinyl stair noses only fit a range of plank thicknesses, and one maker's fits 2–7.5 mm, so check yours. One cost guide puts nosing at roughly $10–$45 per piece, depending on type.

    Glue-down treads with flush nosing vs overlap

    Flush nosing on glued treads

    Glue a shim to the front edge of each step, glue the tread plank behind it, then glue the nose so it butts tight to the plank. One maker leaves about 1.7 in at the front for the shim and nose, and says flush nosing only works on glued treads with the pad removed.

    Overlap nosing

    Glue the tread plank, then glue the nose over its front edge. No shim, and the nose hides small cutting errors. It adds a slight lip at the edge. It is the more forgiving pick for a first staircase.

    Removing carpet first

    1. Start at the top — cut the carpet into one-step pieces with a utility knife and pull it off the risers and treads with locking pliers.
    2. Pad and tack strips — remove the pad, then pry up tack strips with a pry bar over a putty knife so you don't gouge the stair. The tacks are sharp, so wear gloves and safety glasses.
    3. Staples — pull every staple with a staple puller or pliers. Hammer down any that break off flush.
    4. Check the stairs — look for loose or squeaky treads, glue, paint, and debris. The surface has to be clean, flat, and solid for adhesive to grip.

    Under carpet, many stairs have rounded bullnose edges or rough treads. Check that your stair nose fits that shape. Some noses work over both bullnose and square edges. Damaged treads should be fixed or replaced first.

    How the install goes (overview)

    1. Measure each step — width and depth, step by step. Cut the nose to width first, then the tread plank to fill behind it.
    2. Riser, then tread — follow your nosing's order. Some sheets have you set the riser first to get the overhang right.
    3. Glue — run adhesive in an S-pattern under the plank and the nose, press firmly, and tape to hold if your sheet says so.
    4. Let it cure — one maker says no walking for at least 4–12 hours depending on the nosing, and 24–48 hours before full traffic. Plan another way up and down.

    Cost per step

    • DIY materials: roughly $20–$80 per step for tread and riser plank, stair nose, and adhesive.
    • Pro install: cost guides put LVP stairs at roughly $50–$180 per step installed. Angi's 2026 range for stair flooring of any material is $11–$160 per step.
    • A 13-step staircase: about $275–$1,015 DIY or $650–$2,300 with a pro. Curved, open-sided, or damaged stairs cost more.

    Stairs cost more per square foot than a floor because every piece is a custom cut. For flat-floor prices, see How much does LVP cost per square foot? All ranges are approximate 2026 US figures.

    DIY difficulty

    Harder than a flat floor. One install guide estimates about 30–60 minutes per step for a DIYer. A straight, closed staircase in good shape is a fair DIY job if you are patient with measuring. Consider a pro for:

    • Curved stairs, winders, or open sides that need return pieces.
    • Loose, cracked, or damaged treads.
    • Stairs that are the only way in or out of a floor, where downtime matters.

    Safety and slip

    • Watch the top and bottom risers. Adding flooring changes step heights where the stairs meet the floor. The IRC (the model code many US towns use) caps riser height at 7 3/4 in, wants nosing to stick out 3/4–1 1/4 in on most stairs, and allows no more than 3/8 in difference between the tallest and shortest riser in a flight. Measure before you buy, and check your local code.
    • Pick a textured, matte plank. Glossy planks are slicker, especially in socks.
    • Wear layer: stairs take heavy wear at the front edge. About 12 mil is the usual home floor, and 20 mil is the pick with dogs. See Is vinyl okay for my room?
    • Keep a solid handrail and good light, and never wax or polish vinyl stairs.
    • Glue every piece. A loose nose or tread is a fall risk. Re-glue anything that moves.

    Tools

    • Carpet removal: utility knife, locking pliers, pry bar, putty knife, staple puller, gloves, and safety glasses.
    • Measuring and cutting: tape, square, utility knife, and a miter saw or plank cutter for clean, repeatable cuts.
    • Gluing: caulk gun, polyurethane construction adhesive (or the one your nosing names), and painter's tape.

    LVP on stairs FAQ

    Can you put vinyl plank flooring on stairs?

    Yes, but not as a floating floor. On stairs, each tread piece, riser, and stair nose is glued down, usually with a polyurethane construction adhesive, and many brands say to remove the attached pad first. Use the stair nose made for your plank and follow its instructions.

    What is the difference between overlap and flush stair nosing?

    Overlap nosing sits on top of the plank at the front edge of the step and covers the cut edge. It is the more forgiving choice and is often used where stairs meet a floating floor. Flush nosing sits level with the plank and usually needs a shim under it and a glued-down tread with no pad. It looks cleaner but needs more exact cuts.

    How much does it cost to put LVP on stairs?

    Roughly $20 to $80 per step for DIY materials (planks, stair nose, and adhesive), and roughly $50 to $180 per step installed by a pro, depending on the nosing, the stair condition, and local labor. A straight 13-step staircase often lands around $650 to $2,300 with a pro. These are approximate 2026 ranges.

    Is installing LVP on stairs a good DIY project?

    It is harder than a flat floor. Every step is measured and cut on its own, everything is glued, and the adhesive needs time to cure before people walk on it. Handy DIYers can do a straight staircase. Curved, open-sided, or damaged stairs are often worth hiring out.

    Is vinyl plank slippery on stairs?

    It can be, especially in socks. Pick a textured, matte plank, keep a sturdy handrail, light the stairs well, and never wax or polish vinyl stairs.
